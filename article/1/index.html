The coin every cell spends

One small molecule pays for nearly everything your cells do.

Right now, every cell in your body is spending energy. A muscle cell is pulling. A nerve cell is pushing salt out through its skin. A liver cell is building a protein, one link at a time. Nearly all of them pay the same way, with a small molecule called ATP.

You never hold much of it. The ATP inside a hard-working muscle lasts only a second or two before it has to be rebuilt. Yet across one day you make and spend an astonishing amount: estimates run from about half your body weight to all of it. During hard work, the total climbs far higher.

This explainer follows ATP from start to finish: what it is, what kind of energy it holds, how it makes things move, and how the air you breathe and the food you eat rebuild it.

Its style is borrowed from Martin C. Martin's What Everyone Should Know About Mechanical Watches, which was itself inspired by Bartosz Ciechanowski's Mechanical Watch. All the figures move. When a figure has a slider or a button, the text tells you what to do with it.

ATP is your cells' everyday energy money. Its full name is adenosine triphosphate.

Think of coins. You earn them in one place and spend them in another, and the same coins go round and round. Cells do the same with ATP. They charge it up in one place and spend it somewhere else, and each molecule makes the trip again and again.

ATP has three parts. At one end is adenine, a flat pair of rings. Next comes ribose, a small sugar. Together those two are called adenosine. Hanging off the sugar is a short tail of three phosphates, each one a phosphorus atom wrapped in oxygen atoms. "Tri" means three.

Look at the tail in the next picture. Press Spend to snap off the last phosphate.

With help from a water molecule, the end phosphate comes off and floats free. What is left has two phosphates, so it is called ADP, adenosine diphosphate. That one small reaction gives off energy the cell can use.

More · Why phosphate?

Phosphates carry negative electric charge. The charge keeps them from slipping out through a cell's oily skin, and it makes them break down only very slowly in plain water. That pairing suits an energy carrier: ATP holds plenty of energy, yet it sits still until a protein helps it react. The chemist Frank Westheimer laid out this argument in 1987.

Now press Recharge. Putting the phosphate back on makes ATP again, but it costs energy, at least as much as the snap gave. Most recharging happens inside small compartments called mitochondria, which parts 5 to 8 visit.

More · Only in people?

No. All cells, from bacteria to plants to people, run on ATP.

ATP was discovered in 1929 by the German chemist Karl Lohmann, and at the same time by Cyrus Fiske and Yellapragada SubbaRow at Harvard. Between 1939 and 1941, Fritz Lipmann showed that it is the cell's general carrier of energy, spent in one reaction after another.

That is the whole loop in one line: ATP is spent to ADP, and ADP is charged back to ATP. The rest of this explainer is about the two halves of that loop.
